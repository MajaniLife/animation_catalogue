# Collaborative & presence motion

Motion that reports **other people**. Not the reader's own input, not the application's state —
the live position, attention and activity of humans sharing the same surface at the same moment.

This family exists because presence is the one thing in an interface that has no static
equivalent. A button's hover state can be described by its end state; a person's attention
cannot. Where they are, whether they are moving, whether they have stopped — that information
only exists as change over time, which means motion is not the presentation layer here, it *is*
the data. Freeze the frame and the information is gone.

Reach for this family when the product's value is that it is shared. A document two people are
editing, a board a team is arranging, a call several people are in. It is the defining idiom of
the Figma / Linear / Notion generation of tools, and the reason those products feel alive in a
way that a single-player equivalent with the same features does not.

Three properties separate it from everything else in this catalogue:

**The driver is the network, not the reader.** Every other family is driven by time, scroll,
pointer or local state. Here the source of truth is a remote peer and an unreliable transport
between you and them. That changes what can go wrong: not jank, but *staleness* — and the
correct response to a stale peer is to say so, not to keep animating a position you no longer
believe.

**Absence is signal.** In every other family, motion stopping means the animation finished. Here
it means a person stopped, and that is information the reader wants. Entries in this family must
distinguish *idle* from *gone* from *disconnected*, because collapsing them makes the interface
lie about who is in the room.

**It has an inherent accessibility cost.** Continuous motion nobody initiated, generated by
other people, cannot be turned off by fixing your own settings. Reduced motion does not cover
it — WCAG 2.2.2 does, past five seconds. Every entry here needs a way to hide collaborators, and
needs to keep the cursor layer away from screen readers, or a peer's idle drift becomes a stream
of announcements.

### Live cursors (`presence.live-cursors`)

- **One line** — other people's pointers appear on the same surface as small labelled arrows,
  gliding rather than jumping.

- **What the reader sees** — Two or three small arrows drift across the document you are
  looking at, each trailing a coloured name tag. They move continuously and slightly behind
  where that person actually is, so a fast diagonal flick arrives as a smooth sweep a beat late
  rather than as a teleport. When someone stops, their arrow settles rather than halting dead.
  The effect is of watching people rather than watching data: pace alone separates someone
  reading from someone hunting, and a peer who has wandered off dims and goes still until they
  come back. Turn the smoothing off and the same information becomes a set of stuttering jumps
  that reads as a broken connection — which is what it literally is.

- **Mechanism** — positions arrive over a network at a throttled, jittered rate (commonly one
  update every 50–120 ms) and are stored as a target. A `requestAnimationFrame` loop moves each
  cursor a fixed fraction of the remaining distance toward its target every frame, writing
  `transform: translate3d()`. The render rate is decoupled from the wire rate, which is the
  whole trick. The catch-up fraction must be corrected for frame duration or the effect runs at
  double speed on a 120 Hz display.

- **Stack** — no library required; roughly twenty lines of vanilla JS over a websocket. A
  spring library — Motion, or Framer Motion — gives better direction changes at the cost of a
  dependency. `perfect-cursors` (MIT) fits a spline through several samples for accurate
  pathing, at the cost of extra latency because it must wait for more than one point.

- **Params** — catch-up fraction per frame, default `0.16`; below `0.05` the cursor lags so far
  it feels disconnected from the name on it, above `0.4` the smoothing stops doing anything.
  Wire rate, default 80 ms; past roughly 250 ms no interpolation rescues it and you should show
  a stale state instead. Cursor size fixed — scaling with zoom makes distant collaborators
  vanish.

- **Use when** — two or more people are looking at one surface at once; when knowing *where*
  someone is looking matters as much as what they changed; when the product's value is that it
  is shared.

- **Don't use when** — presence is incidental rather than the point; when a session may exceed
  roughly a dozen simultaneous users without culling, at which point cursors become noise; when
  the surface scrolls independently per user, because a cursor at another person's scroll
  offset is meaningless without a shared coordinate space.

- **Reduced motion** — cursors remain and continue to report position, applying each received
  update directly with no glide. Presence information survives intact; only the smoothing is
  dropped. This is one of the few effects where the reduced-motion state is *less* comfortable
  than the animated one, and it is still correct: the motion is a comfort, the position is the
  information. Separately from reduced motion, this runs continuously without the reader having
  started it, so WCAG 2.2.2 applies past five seconds: ship a hide-collaborators toggle and
  persist it.

- **Performance** — compositor-only. One `transform` write per cursor per frame, no layout, no
  paint. Cost scales linearly with simultaneous users, so cull to the visible viewport and cap
  the rendered count. The expensive part is never the animation; it is the message rate, and
  throttling the wire is what protects both.

- **Gotchas** — seeding is the one that bites: if the first sample arrives before the container
  has been laid out, every cursor seeds at the origin and visibly flies in from the top-left
  corner on load. Guard on a non-zero container size. Interpolating in page coordinates breaks
  the moment two users have different scroll offsets or zoom — send document-space coordinates.
  Easing each update with `ease-in-out` produces a distinctive slow-fast-slow pulse per packet;
  linear is correct here precisely because the motion is continuous. Name labels must not
  intercept pointer events or they steal clicks from the document beneath. A touch user sees
  every remote cursor but emits none of their own, having no persistent pointer to broadcast —
  surface them as present-but-not-pointing rather than letting them read as absent, and never
  gate the surface on having a cursor. Mark the cursor layer `aria-hidden` and announce
  presence changes through a polite live region, or a screen reader narrates every peer
  movement.
